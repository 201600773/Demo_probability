    4 · Why this matters

    The house edge is small. The number of hands is enormous. A casino's advantage on blackjack is under 1%. On one hand that is invisible. Across ten million hands it is a certainty.
    A poll of 1,000 can predict 40 million votes. Not because 1,000 is a large fraction, but because noise shrinks with the square root of the sample.
    A trial of 12 patients tells you almost nothing. Small trials produce dramatic, publishable, wrong results all the time. That is the mechanism behind the replication crisis.

    At Least One

    The single most common arithmetic error in everyday probability.

    "Roll a fair die four times. What is the probability of getting at least one 6?"

    Nearly every adult answers 4/6 ≈ 67%. The correct answer is 51.8%. The gap is the whole module.

    1 · The rule

    Something either happens or it doesn't. So the two probabilities must add to 1.

    P(not A) = 1 − P(A)
    Equivalently: P(A) + P(not A) = 1

    This is the complement rule. It sounds trivial. It is the entire engine.

    2 · The trap

    Roll a fair die four times. P(at least one 6)?

    6 · Why this matters

    "One in a million" happens every day. Zambia has 20 million people. A 1-in-a-million daily event should be expected roughly twenty times a day. Coincidences are not rare once you multiply over opportunities.
    Pooled testing saves money. Pool 20 samples. If negative, all 20 are clear — one test instead of twenty. If prevalence is 1%, P(at least one positive in the pool) = 1 − 0.99²⁰ ≈ 18%. That 18% is the price of the 82% saving.
    Redundancy in engineering. Two independent engines failing: 1 − (1−f)². The complement is how safety is engineered.

    The Two Rules

    When to add probabilities, when to multiply, and how to tell the difference.

    Almost every probability mistake traces back to using the wrong one of two rules.

    1 · The rules

    OR: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B) If A and B cannot both happen: P(A)+P(B) AND: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A) If A and B are independent: P(A) × P(B)

    4 · Why the rules differ

    OR adds. AND multiplies. Adding counts the union — everything in either circle. Multiplying counts the overlap — only what's in both. Venn diagrams make this obvious; formulas hide it.
    Without replacement, trials are not independent. After drawing one red, the bag has 4 red out of 9. Conditional probability is how you track the shift — that's Module 4.

    Bayes, the Human Way

    Why a "95% accurate" test for a rare disease is usually wrong.

    A disease affects 1% of people. A test is 95% sensitive and has a 5% false-positive rate. You test positive. What's the chance you actually have it?

    1 · The natural-frequency view

    Imagine 10,000 people. Draw them. This is the version of Bayes that humans actually grasp.

    2 · The number that matters

    P(disease | positive) = 95 / (95 + 495) = 0.161

    Yes — a positive result on a "95% accurate" test gives you a 16% chance of disease. Because 99 out of 100 people don't have it, and 5% of those healthy people false-alarm.

    3 · Turn the dials

    1.0%
    95%
    95%

    4 · Why this matters

    Mass screening for rare conditions does this every day. Even a good test on a rare disease produces mostly false positives. This is why mammograms at 40 in low-risk women are debated.
    Bayes is how belief updates. Your prior times the likelihood of evidence, divided by the total probability of the evidence. That's it. Bayes' theorem is the complement rule plus the multiplication rule.

    Monty Hall

    Three doors. The most famous counterintuition in probability.

    You pick a door. The host — who knows where the prize is — opens a different door with a goat. Do you switch, or stay?

    1 · Play it

    Pick a door. The host will open a goat door. Then decide.

    3 · Prove it

    Simulate 10,000 games with each strategy.

    4 · Why switching wins

    Your first pick wins 1/3 of the time. Nothing the host does changes that. So the other unopened door must win the remaining 2/3.
    The host's choice carries information. He cannot open the prize door. If you picked wrong (2/3 chance), the door he leaves closed is the winner. Switching flips your win probability from 1/3 to 2/3.

    Expected Value

    The one number that tells you what will happen if you keep playing.

    Roll a fair die. If it shows the face value, you win that many kwacha. It costs 3 to play. Should you?

    1 · The math

    E[X] = Σ p(x) · x
    = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.50
    Net = 3.50 − 3.00 = +0.50 per game

    Positive — you should play. On average. Over many games.

    3 · Real-world EVs

    Every casino game has negative EV — for you. That is the entire business model. The house does not need to win any given hand; it only needs a positive expected value and enough hands.
    Insurance has negative EV for you and that's fine. You are not buying expected value. You are buying the removal of a tail-risk outcome you cannot absorb. EV is not the same as rationality.
    Investing is EV versus variance. Two strategies with the same EV can be radically different in what they do to your life.

    Fallacies & Traps

    Three errors that catch smart people repeatedly. Learn to see them.

    1 · The gambler's fallacy

    A fair roulette wheel has landed on red five times in a row. What is the chance the next spin is black?

    2 · The conjunction fallacy

    Linda is 31, outspoken, and very bright. She studied philosophy. As a student she was deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice.

    Which is more likely?

    3 · The base-rate fallacy

    In a city of 100,000, one person in 1,000 has a rare disease. A test is 99% accurate (99% sensitivity, 99% specificity). You test positive. What's the chance you actually have it?
